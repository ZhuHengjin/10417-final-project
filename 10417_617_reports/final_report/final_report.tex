\begin{abstract}
We propose a semantic-aware weighting for contrastive representation distillation (CRD) to guide the student model in distinguishing visually similar inputs while aligning with the teacher’s embeddings. Our method emphasizes informative negative pairs based on the teacher’s similarity structure. Experiments on CIFAR-100 show consistent improvements over standard CRD. Further analysis suggests the gain arises from better generalization ability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%Nachuan
Knowledge distillation has emerged as an effective strategy for transferring information from a large, well-trained teacher network to a smaller student network, boosting the performance of small models. Traditional distillation methods, such as logit-based training, primarily focus on matching output distributions but  overlook information on structural relationships of feature representations. 

Contrastive Representation Distillation (CRD) extends the idea of distilling knowledge beyond output logits by explicitly modeling relationships in the feature space. Instead of only encouraging the student to mimic the teacher’s predictions, CRD aligns the teacher’s hidden representations with that of the student. This is achieved through a contrastive objective that treats pairs of teacher–student features from the same image  as positive samples, while pairs drawn from different classes are treated as negatives. In other words, CRD encourages the student to be close to the teacher in representation space when their corresponding inputs share the same label, and to be pushed apart otherwise. This allows CRD to encode class-level structural relationships rather than relying solely on output logits.

However, the original CRD formulation assumes all cross-class pairs are equally negative. This ignores the fact that samples from different classes may still exhibit nontrivial semantic correlations—for example,  visually similar objects (e.g., dolphin vs. whale). The standard contrastive loss pushes all negative pairs apart with equal force, regardless of whether the teacher’s hidden representations consider them mildly similar or fundamentally different.

To address this limitation, we propose a semantic-weighted CRD (SW-CRD) approach that incorporates teacher-side semantic similarity directly into the contrastive loss. Instead of treating all negative pairs equally, we compute a similarity score between the teacher’s hidden states for each pair of inputs and use this score as a weight in the loss. Pairs that the teacher think to be more similar impose a stronger repulsive force, while dissimilar pairs impose a weaker one. This weighting scheme allows the student to capture not only the class-level structure but also the finer-grained relational geometry embedded in the teacher’s representation space.

\section{Related Work}
%Nachuan
Knowledge distillation (KD), introduced by \citet{hinton2015distilling}, transfers knowledge from a large teacher network to a smaller student network by encouraging the student to match the teacher’s soft output probabilities, which has been widely used to boost the performance of smaller models. Building on this idea, Contrastive Representation Distillation (CRD) \citep{crd_tian2022crd} aligns student and teacher embeddings via a contrastive loss, which allows the student to learn knowledge in the hidden states of the teacher, rather than learning only the output.  

Boosting Contrastive Learning with Relation Knowledge Distillation (ReKD) \citep{RKDCL_zheng2021boostingcontrastivelearningrelation} enhanced the CRD by modeling pairwise relationships between features in the teacher’s representation space, transferring structural knowledge to the student. However, it treats all relations equally and does not account for the relevance of relations. 

To incorporate semantics, Generating and Weighting Semantically Consistent Sample Pairs for Ultrasound Contrastive Learning \citep{semantic_chen2022generatingweightingsemanticallyconsistent} weights contrastive pairs based on semantic consistency, showing that emphasizing semantically meaningful pairs improves representation learning. 

In this project, we wish to utilize the power of semantic information in the field of knowledge distillation and propose Semantic-Aware Relational Contrastive Distillation, which adds a semantic-based weight on top of CRD.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Semantic Weighted Loss}

In CRD, the distillation objective is formulated as a lower bound of the mutual information (MI) between teacher and student representations. For a parametric critic function $h(T,S)$, the original contrastive loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{critic}}(h) =
    \mathbb{E}_{q(T,S|C=1)}[\log h(T,S)]
    +
    N,\mathbb{E}_{q(T,S|C=0)}[\log(1 - h(T,S))],
\end{equation}
where $q(T,S|C=1)$ denote the joint distribution and $q(T,S|C=0)$ denote the product-of-marginals distributions for positive and negative pairs, respectively. All negative pairs are treated as identically distributed background samples.

However, the teacher representation typically exhibits non-uniform geometric structure, where negative samples vary substantially in semantic proximity to the anchor. Uniform negative sampling therefore leads to suboptimal MI estimation because the critic allocates equal weight to both informative (nearby) and trivial (far-apart) negatives.

Intuitively, negatives that are more semantically similar to the anchor (as measured by the teacher’s embeddings) should exert a stronger influence on the loss, encouraging the student to learn finer-grained distinctions. Conversely, dissimilar negatives can be down-weighted since they are less informative.

To incorporate this structural information, we introduce a *semantic-weighted critic* in which the contribution of each negative pair is modulated by a teacher-defined weight, over negative samples in the same domain $w(T,S) : \mathcal{T} \times \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. We define it using teacher features:
\begin{equation}
w(T,S)
=
1 + \alpha_{\text{SW}} \exp \left( \frac{\cos(f_t(x_T), f_t(x_S))}{\tau_{\text{SW}}} \right).
\label{def:semantic-weights}
\end{equation}

Here $x_T$ and $x_S$ are the underlying samples that produced $T$ and $S$. Also $\alpha_{\text{SW}}$ controls the strength of semantic modulation and $\tau_{\text{SW}}$ is a temperature that adjusts sensitivity to similarity. This weight increases for teacher-near negative samples and smoothly decays for distant ones. The modified contrastive objective becomes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}(h)
=
\mathbb{E}_{q(T,S|C=1)}[\log h(T,S)]
+
N \cdot \mathbb{E}_{q(T,S|C=0)}\big[
w(T, S) \cdot \log(1 - h(T,S))
\big].
\end{equation}

Although we introduce a semantic weighting term $w(T,S)$ to modulate the negative sampling distribution, we show in Appendix A that the resulting objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}$ remains a valid variational lower bound on $I(T;S)$. The weighting simply rescales the implicit negative distribution, tightening the bound in regions where discrimination is most challenging.

Although we introduce a semantic weighting term $w(T,S)$ to modulate the negative sampling distribution, we show in Appendix~\ref{app:mi-lower-bound} (Proposition~\ref{prop:mi-lower-bound}) that the resulting objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}$ remains a valid variational lower bound on $I(T;S)$.

Intuitively, standard CRD treats all negatives as equally informative despite strong geometric structure in teacher embeddings. Our weight $w(T,S)$ increases the contribution of semantically close negatives, concentrating probability mass on “hard” regions of the teacher manifold. This reweighted negative distribution shifts the MI lower bound by a constant $C = \log N + \mathbb{E}_{pos}[\log w]$, forcing the student to learn fine-grained distinctions ignored by uniform sampling.


\subsubsection{Memory Buffer Implementation for the Semantic Weights}
We inherit the contrastive memory buffer architecture of CRD \citep{crd_tian2022crd} and keep the same large-$N$ regime enabled by a memory bank. Concretely, our $\texttt{ContrastMemory}$ module maintains two buffers for student and teacher features $(\texttt{memory\_v1}, \texttt{memory\_v2})$ and uses an alias sampler to draw $K$ negatives plus one positive per anchor, exactly as in the original implementation.

On top of this, we compute semantic weights $w(T,S)$ by reusing the teacher buffer: for each teacher anchor we retrieve the corresponding teacher negatives from $\texttt{memory\_v2}$, form their cosine similarities, and transform them with the $\alpha,\tau$ parameterization in Eq.~\ref{def:semantic-weights} to obtain per-negative weights.

Since these weights are computed only on the sampled memory entries and share the same batched matrix-vector operations as the original score computation, our semantic-weighted loss adds almost no extra computational or memory cost while fully preserving the benefit of a large implicit negative set.

\textbf{Computational Overhead:} With $N = 4096$ negatives, both CRD and SW-CRD require roughly 20 seconds per training epoch, indicating that semantic weighting introduces virtually no additional cost at this scale. When increasing $N$ to 16384, however, a noticeable gap emerges: CRD trains at approximately 25 seconds per epoch, whereas SW-CRD reaches about 30 seconds per epoch. This suggests that, while the method remains efficient in the common operating regime, our current implementation may exhibit limited scalability at very large $N$, leaving room for future optimization. (Device details provided in Section~\ref{device}.)


\subsection{Knowledge Distillation Objective}

Knowledge Distillation \citep{hinton2015distilling}, set the core framework for transferring knowledge from a large teacher model $T$ to a small student model $S$. 
The total loss in KD is defined as a weighted combination of the cross-entropy loss and the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between the output distributions of the teacher and student:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = (1 - \alpha)\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(y, p_S) + \alpha T^2 \, \text{KL}(p_T \| p_S)
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter that balances the two loss terms, and $p_T$ and $p_S$ denote teacher and student outputs.

\subsection{Ensemble Distillation Loss}

In the \texttt{crd\_sw} configuration, training uses a single-teacher objective per batch. The student is supervised by three terms: (i) a cross-entropy loss between student logits and ground-truth labels, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(S,y)$; (ii) a logit-based distillation loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(S,T; \tau_{\text{kd}})$ implemented as a KL divergence at temperature $\tau_{\text{kd}}$; and (iii) a semantic-weighted CRD loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}(S,T)$ computed between the last-layer features of the teacher and student. The total objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
  =
  \gamma\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(S,y)
  +
  \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(S,T; T_{\text{kd}})
  +
  \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}(S,T).
  \label{eq:totla-loss}
\end{equation}

During the main experiments, we set $\alpha = 0$, effectively removing the logit-based KD term. This allows us to focus on feature-based distillation via $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}$ and isolate the contribution of our semantic-weighted contrastive objective.


\section{Experiment Setup}
% Nachuan
\subsection{Dataset}
We conduct our experiments with the CIFAR-100 dataset, a widely used benchmark in computer vision and representation learning. CIFAR-100 consists of 60,000 color images of size 32×32, split into 50,000 training images and 10,000 test images. Each image belongs to one of 100 fine-grained classes, such as apple, forest, oak tree, bus, or whale. The dataset also groups these 100 classes into 20 superclasses, though our experiments operate at the fine-class level.

In our preliminary experiment on vanilla Knowledge Distillation baseline (KD) and Relational Knowledge Distillation (RKD), we use the whole training set. For CRD, we use a customized dataset class based on CIFAR-100. In addition to loading images and labels, the dataset precomputes a set of negative indices (images from all other classes). For each training sample, the dataset contains, the image, its label, and also a list of sampled indices consisting of the image itself as a positive sample and $k$ negative examples. These sampled indices are used to construct contrastive pairs for losses. 

\subsection{Implementation Detail}
All experiments were done using the RepDistiller framework\footnote{\url{https://github.com/HobbitLong/RepDistiller}}
, which provides standardized implementations of a wide range of knowledge distillation methods, including CRD. We used the official codebase as our starting point and incorporated several modifications to support our proposed similarity-weighted contrastive distillation loss. 

For the teacher model, we utilized a pretrained model given by the RepDistiller as we intend to compare the performance of our method with the benchmark. The student model is a ResNet-32, following the architecture and hyperparameters commonly used in CRD benchmarks. All experiments were performed on the CIFAR-100 dataset, which contains 50,000 training images and 10,000 test images across 100 categories.

Following the original CRD setup, training was performed for 240 epochs using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum 0.9 and weight decay set to $5\times10^{-4}$. we used a batch size of 64 and adopted the standard multi-step learning rate schedule, where the learning rate starts at 0.05 and is decayed by a factor of 10 at epochs 150, 180, and 210. We also kept consistent with the defaults in the official implementation of CRD for temperature of $0.07$ and projection head dimensions at $128$.

To implement our similarity-weighted CRD (SW-CRD) method, we modified the contrastive module to compute teacher feature similarity for each mini-batch. Specifically, after extracting the teacher’s hidden representations, we compute the cosine similarity between all teacher feature pairs and use this as a multiplicative weight on the contrastive loss for negative pairs. 

All experiments were run on an AWS g5.xlarge instance, which has 24 GB GPU memory, 4 vCPUs, and 16 GB memory. \label{device}

\subsection{Hyperparameters}
In addition to the fixed hyperparameters described in the Implementation Detail section (e.g., learning rate schedule, batch size, optimizer settings), our method introduces several additional hyperparameters that were tuned to optimize performance. These hyperparameters control aspects of the similarity-weighted contrastive distillation (SW-CRD) loss:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Number of Negatives} $N$: This determines the number of negative pairs sampled for each anchor in the contrastive loss. A larger $N$ provides a more comprehensive view of the feature space and can improve representation learning, but it also increases memory and computational requirements, and is subject to overfitting. We experimented with several values to balance efficiency and performance.
    \item \textbf{Temperature of Semantic Aware Similarity} $\tau_{\text{SW}}$ (Eq.~\ref{def:semantic-weights}): This temperature controls the sharpness of the similarity weighting applied to the teacher’s feature pairs. Lower values of $\tau_{\text{SW}}$ make the weighting distribution more peaky, emphasizing high-similarity pairs, whereas higher values produce a softer weighting that treats different similarity levels more evenly. Proper tuning of $\tau_{\text{SW}}$ is important to ensure that the student preserves subtle inter-class relationships without overemphasizing minor similarities.  
    \item \textbf{Scale of weights} $\alpha_{\text{SW}}$ (Eq.~\ref{def:semantic-weights}): This hyperparameter scales the contribution of the semantic-aware weighting in the overall contrastive loss. Setting $\alpha_{\text{SW}}=0$ reduces our method to the standard CRD loss, while larger values increase the influence of teacher-assigned pairwise similarity. Tuning $\alpha_{\text{SW}}$ allows us to find a balance between strictly following the original contrastive objective and leveraging nuanced similarity information.  
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Number of Negatives $N$:}
We evaluate SW-CRD with different numbers of sampled negatives $N \in \{64, 256, 1024, 4096, 16384\}$. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:N-tuning}, both top-1 and top-5 accuracies generally improve as $N$ increases, confirming that a larger pool of negatives in the memory bank is beneficial. Compared with the CRD paper \citep{crd_tian2022crd}, where increasing $N$ from $4096$ to $16384$ yields less than a $0.1\%$ gain, our setting with the sematic weight is more sensitive in this regime: in our experiments, the improvement from $N = 4096$ to $N = 16384$ is around $0.3$ percentage points for both top-1 and top-5 accuracy. To exploit this additional benefit, we adopt $N = 16384$ negatives in all main experiments. ($N$ is referred to as $\texttt{nce\_k}$ in the codebase.)

\textbf{Temperature $\tau_{\text{SW}}$:}
In Figure~\ref{fig:sematic-tau-tuning}, both top-1 and top-5 accuracy attain their maximum at $\tau_{\text{SW}} = 0.5$. The top-1 curve is more sensitive to the temperature, showing a noticeable drop once $\tau_{\text{SW}}$ exceeds 0.5, whereas the top-5 accuracy remains relatively stable and varies only mildly across settings. The top-5 curve nevertheless forms a smooth hill, increasing as $\tau_{\text{SW}}$ approaches 0.5 and decreasing for larger or smaller values, indicating that very low or very high temperatures are slightly suboptimal. Based on this trend, we fix $\tau_{\text{SW}} = 0.5$ in all subsequent experiments.

\textbf{Scale of weights $\alpha_{\text{SW}}$:}
Figure~\ref{fig:sematic-alpha-tuning} shows that both top-1 and top-5 accuracy achieve their highest values at $\alpha_{\text{SW}} = 1.0$. The accuracy curves form a smooth, unimodal hill: performance increases steadily as $\alpha_{\text{SW}}$ approaches 1.0, then declines symmetrically as $\alpha_{\text{SW}}$ moves away from this point. This behavior suggests that $\alpha_{\text{SW}} = 1.0$ is a well-calibrated and optimal weighting for our semantic weighting term. Therefore, we adopt $\alpha_{\text{SW}} = 1.0$ for all subsequent experiments.

We also include the case $\alpha_{\text{SW}} = -1$, which corresponds to an alternative, inverted interpretation of our proposed weighting scheme. Instead of forcing the student to separate embeddings when the teacher produces closely clustered representations for different classes, the negative value effectively pushes the student to resemble the teachers' similarities. The resulting performance drop clearly indicates that this inverted interpretation is ineffective, reinforcing that the original formulation of the semantic weighting term is the correct one.

\section{Discussion and Analysis}

\subsection{Overfitting v.s. Generalization}

Taken together, the patterns in Figure~\ref{fig:acc_close} show that the performance gain of SW-CRD stems from improved generalization rather than stronger memorization. Semantic weighting substantially reshapes the contrastive objective by amplifying teacher-similar negatives, yet it leaves training accuracy essentially unchanged, indicating that the student is not fitting the training data more tightly but is learning more semantically meaningful relationships. This effect is reflected in the consistently higher test accuracy of SW-CRD, even when training accuracy shows no improvement: the model becomes better at separating visually similar inputs (such as images within the same superclass) without overfitting. The stable and repeated margin on the test curve across many consecutive epochs therefore demonstrates that the improvement is systematic and genuinely generalizable, not a product of noise or random fluctuation.

\subsection{Strengths and Limitations}

\textbf{Strengths.}
SW-CRD offers a lightweight, drop-in extension to CRD that preserves the original memory-buffer computation while delivering consistent test-time gains. By incorporating semantic information from the teacher’s representation space, the method improves the student’s ability to distinguish visually similar inputs without increasing training accuracy or computational cost. This makes the approach attractive for scenarios where improved generalization is desired but training budgets are fixed.

\textbf{Limitations.}
Despite its strong empirical behavior, the method introduces additional hyperparameters whose effects are nontrivial to tune. Because semantic weighting depends directly on the teacher’s representation geometry, any spurious structure in the teacher embeddings can be amplified by the weighting mechanism. Furthermore, pushing the student away from teacher-near negatives risks conflicting with the goal of distillation, and our experiments show that poorly chosen weights can degrade performance below baseline CRD. Finally, our current evaluation is limited to a single teacher–student pair on CIFAR-100, so the method’s scalability to larger models, alternative architectures, and more complex datasets remains uncertain.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../../report/graphs/stacked_losses.png}
    \caption{Loss decomposition during training with both SW-CRD and KL distillation.}
    \label{fig:stacked-losses}

To understand whether logit-based distillation provides additional benefit when semantic-weighted contrastive learning is used, we conduct an ablation where KL term is included alongside SW-CRD with $\alpha = 1$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:totla-loss}. Figure X visualizes the loss decomposition and shows the contribution to the total loss from each term during training, with KL providing a smaller but steady contribution. This indicates that, once the initial transient phase is over, both terms are optimized in a relatively balanced way rather than one completely vanishing.

However, the empirical results indicate that including KL actually slightly degrades performance. With KL activated, the model achieves 75.99\% top-1 accuracy, compared to 76.01\% when trained with SW-CRD alone. Test loss is also noticeably higher (0.9527 vs. 0.8525). This suggests that KL interacts suboptimally with the SW-CRD objective: once the student is strongly guided by feature-space contrastive alignment, the softened-output matching imposed by KL may introduce conflicting gradients or unnecessary regularization.

\subsection{Future Work}

Future research should examine SW-CRD across broader image benchmarks and larger, more diverse teacher–student configurations to better understand its robustness and scalability. Integrating semantic weighting with logit-based or feature-based KD variants may provide complementary benefits. Additionally, exploring alternative formulations of the weighting function. Such as adaptive schemes that automatically tune the influence of teacher similarity or regularizers that prevent over-separation from the teacher could help stabilize performance. Finally, a deeper investigation into the geometric properties of teacher representations may reveal when semantic weighting is most beneficial and how to mitigate its failure modes.

\section{Mutual Information Lower Bound for SW-CRD}
\label{app:mi-lower-bound}

For notational simplicity, we denote the joint distribution of positive pairs by $p(T,S)$ and the product of marginals (for negatives) by $p(T)p(S)$, corresponding to $q(T,S \mid C=1)$ and $q(T,S \mid C=0)$ in the main text.

\subsection{Optimal Semantic-Weighted Critic}

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:optimal-critic}
  The optimal critic function $h^*(T,S)$ that maximizes the semantic-weighted objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}$ is given by:
  $$
  h^*(T,S) = \frac{p(T,S)}{p(T,S) + N \cdot w(T,S) \cdot p(T)p(S)}
  $$
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The semantic-weighted objective function is defined as:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}(h) = \mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)}[\log h(T,S)] + N \cdot \mathbb{E}_{p(T)p(S)}\big[ w(T,S) \cdot \log(1 - h(T,S)) \big]
    \end{equation}
    We can express the expectations as integrals over the domains of $T$ and $S$:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}(h) = \iint \Big( p(T,S) \log h(T,S) + N \cdot w(T,S) p(T)p(S) \log(1 - h(T,S)) \Big) \, dT \, dS
    \end{equation}
    To maximize the functional $\mathcal{L}(h)$ with respect to the function $h$, it is sufficient to maximize the integrand pointwise for every pair $(T,S)$. Let $y = h(T,S)$ be the value of the critic at a specific pair. We define the scalar objective function $J(y)$ for this fixed pair as:
    \begin{equation}
        J(y) = A \cdot \log(y) + B \cdot \log(1 - y)
    \end{equation}
    where the coefficients $A$ and $B$ are non-negative constants specific to the pair $(T,S)$:
    \begin{equation}
        A = p(T,S), \quad B = N \cdot w(T,S) \cdot p(T)p(S)
    \end{equation}
    To find the optimal value $y^*$, we take the derivative of $J(y)$ with respect to $y$ and set it to zero:
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{dJ}{dy} = \frac{A}{y} - \frac{B}{1 - y} = 0
    \end{equation}
    Solving for $y$:
    \begin{equation}
    \frac{A}{y} = \frac{B}{1 - y} 
    \implies
    y = \frac{A}{A + B}
    \end{equation}
    We substitute the definitions of $A$ and $B$ back into the expression for $y$:
    \begin{equation}
    h^*(T,S) = \frac{p(T,S)}{p(T,S) + N \cdot w(T,S) \cdot p(T)p(S)}
    \end{equation}
    To confirm this is a maximum, we observe the second derivative:
    \begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2J}{dy^2} = -\frac{A}{y^2} - \frac{B}{(1-y)^2}
    \end{equation}
    Since $A, B \ge 0$ and $y \in (0,1)$, the second derivative is strictly negative, confirming that $h^*(T,S)$ is the global maximum.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Mutual Information Lower Bound}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:mi-lower-bound}
    Maximizing the semantic-weighted critic objective $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}(h)$ with respect to the student encoder $f_S$ maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information $I(T;S)$ between teacher and student representations.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Let $p(T,S)$ denote the joint distribution and $p(T)p(S)$ the product of marginals. The objective is:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}(h) = \mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)}[\log h(T,S)] + N \mathbb{E}_{p(T)p(S)}[w(T,S) \log(1 - h(T,S))].
    \end{equation}
    By pointwise maximization, the optimal critic $h^*$ for a fixed encoder is given by:
    \begin{equation}
        h^*(T,S) = \frac{p(T,S)}{p(T,S) + N w(T,S) p(T)p(S)}.
    \end{equation}
    We relate this optimal critic to the mutual information. Considering $\log h^*(T,S)$:
    \begin{equation}
        \log h^*(T,S) 
        = \log \left( \frac{1}{1 + \frac{N w(T,S) p(T)p(S)}{p(T,S)}} \right) 
        = - \log \left( 1 + \frac{N w(T,S) p(T)p(S)}{p(T,S)} \right).
    \end{equation}
    Using the inequality $-\log(1+x) \leq -\log(x)$ for $x>0$:
    \begin{equation}
        \log h^*(T,S) 
        \leq \log \left( \frac{p(T,S)}{p(T)p(S)} \right) - \log(N) - \log w(T,S).
    \end{equation}
    Taking the expectation over the joint distribution $p(T,S)$:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)}[\log h^*(T,S)] 
        \leq \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)} \left[ \log \frac{p(T,S)}{p(T)p(S)} \right]}_{I(T;S)} - \log N - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)}[\log w(T,S)]}_{K}.
    \end{equation}
    Rearranging for $I(T;S)$:
    \begin{equation}
        I(T;S) \geq \mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)}[\log h^*] + \log N + K.
    \end{equation}
    Since $\log(1-h^*) < 0$ and $w(T,S) \geq 0$, the term $N \mathbb{E}_{p(T)p(S)}[w \log(1-h^*)]$ is non-positive. Adding it to the RHS maintains the lower bound:
    \begin{equation}
        I(T;S) \geq \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(T,S)}[\log h^*] + N \mathbb{E}_{p(T)p(S)}[w \log(1-h^*)]}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}}(h^*)} + \log N + K.
    \end{equation}
    Thus, $I(T;S) \geq \mathcal{L}_{\text{SW-CRD}} + C$, where $C$ is a constant independent of the student encoder.
\end{proof}
